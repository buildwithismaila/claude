Materials and energy bands

Metal–semiconductor interface (contact): the boundary formed where a metal touches a semiconductor. Electrons move across it until both share one Fermi level.

Metal: a solid with a very large density of free electrons (10²⁸–10²⁹ per m³), so it conducts electricity very easily.

Semiconductor: a solid whose conductivity lies between that of a metal and an insulator, and can be controlled by doping, temperature and light. Examples: Si, Ge, GaAs.

Doping: adding a small, controlled amount of impurity atoms. Donors (e.g. phosphorus) give free electrons: n-type. Acceptors (e.g. boron) create holes: p-type.

Majority carrier: the charge carrier present in larger numbers: electrons in n-type, holes in p-type material.

Energy band: a range of electron energies that is allowed in a solid. Between bands lie energies no electron can have.

Conduction and valence bands: the lowest band that is normally empty (its electrons move freely), and the highest band that is normally full (its empty places are holes).

Band gap: the energy between the bottom of the conduction band and the top of the valence band. No electron states lie in it. Silicon: 1.12 eV.

These four slides define every concept used in the talk, so anyone can follow. Start with the interface itself: the boundary where a metal touches a semiconductor. Electrons move across it until the two share a single Fermi level. A metal has an enormous number of free electrons; a semiconductor's conductivity is controlled by doping. Donors give electrons and make n-type material; acceptors give holes and make p-type. Electrons in a solid can only have energies inside allowed bands. The conduction band is normally empty and the valence band normally full; the gap between them, 1.12 eV in silicon, has no electron states.
