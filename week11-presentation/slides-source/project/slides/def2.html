Energy levels: how we measure electrons

Electron-volt (eV): the energy an electron gains moving through 1 volt. 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. All energies in this talk are in eV.

Vacuum level: the energy of an electron at rest just outside the material, free of it. Every other energy is measured from this level.

Fermi level: the energy at which an electron state has a 50 % chance of being occupied. At equilibrium it is flat: the same everywhere in materials that touch.

Equilibrium: no applied voltage and no net current; nothing changes with time.

Work function: the minimum energy needed to take an electron from the Fermi level to the vacuum level. For a semiconductor it changes with doping. Gold: 5.10 eV.

Electron affinity: the energy from the bottom of the conduction band up to the vacuum level. It is fixed for each material. Silicon: 4.05 eV.

Thermal energy, kT: the energy scale of random thermal motion, Boltzmann’s constant times the temperature: 0.0259 eV at 300 K.

Energies are given in electron-volts. The vacuum level is our zero: an electron at rest just outside the material. The Fermi level is the 50 percent occupation level, and the key rule of the topic is that at equilibrium it is flat. The work function is the energy from the Fermi level up to the vacuum level; the electron affinity is from the bottom of the conduction band up to the vacuum level. The difference matters: doping moves the Fermi level, so it changes the semiconductor's work function but not its electron affinity. kT, 0.0259 eV at room temperature, is the yardstick for whether a barrier is large.
